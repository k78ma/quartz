_There is a certain enchanting magic that comes from working with robots; by waving our hands, we can make machines seem alive._ Like many, I was drawn to robotics because of this magic. But as robots such as autonomous vehicles approach real-world deployment at scale, that magic must be matched by reliability. Across projects in grasping, industrial vehicles, and autonomous driving, I’ve observed the same pattern: learning-based systems are powerful but fragile, while safety-critical robots require both strong generalization and verifiable safety. This drives my goal of building robotic systems that are not only capable, but also safe and useful in the messy, long-tailed real world. 

My first experience grappling with reliability in high-stakes settings came from medical imaging rather than robotics. Under Prof. Pengcheng Xi and Prof. Alexander Wong, I worked on combining self-supervised learning, imbalance-aware loss functions, and trustworthiness quantification to build COVID-19 diagnosis models. This project resulted in several papers \cite{trudlmia} \cite{icml2022} \cite{cvis2022}, and our system was deployed across 15 hospitals. Beyond introducing me to deep learning research, it taught me how to handle limited data, long-tailed behavior, and the demands of dependable performance in real-world environments.

I carried these skills into bin-picking research with Prof. Wong, where I experimented with Mixture-of-Experts methods to train specialist models for rare object categories and corner cases. Even in this highly controlled setting, using mostly synthetic data and simulation, the process often felt like an endless game of whack-a-mole. Around the same time, I joined WATonomous, Waterloo’s student autonomous-driving team supervised by Dr. Mohammad Al-Sharman, where I worked on trajectory prediction and 3D scene reconstruction for our full-scale self-driving vehicle. Both projects faced similar challenges: modeling uncertainty, handling temporal dynamics, and managing real-world scene complexity at scale, even in the known environment of a test track. Together, these early experiences in robotics research left me with a deep question: if achieving robustness was this difficult even in controlled settings, how could robots ever operate safely in the open world?

Seeking to answer this question, I joined Polymath Robotics, a startup building autonomous heavy industrial vehicles. There, I implemented and trained 3D perception models, but data scarcity and large domain gaps across vehicle platforms often forced us to fall back on simpler heuristics – for example, treating any point above a height threshold as an obstacle. This contrast between sophisticated models and pragmatic heuristics demonstrated the gap between research-grade perception and deployable autonomy. I later returned to contribute to Polymath’s ISO 26262–aligned functional safety stack, which gave me first-hand exposure to the engineering rigor required for production autonomy. I also saw that learning-based approaches, while powerful, were often not sufficient on their own; real deployments demand an integration of classical safety architectures with modern data-driven components.

I saw this same interplay between learning-based capability and classical safety engineering during my two internships at NVIDIA, where I combined my perception and safety background to support the active safety system for the Mercedes-Benz autonomous driving program. Unlike my earlier research work, which focused primarily on improving model capability, or my work at Polymath, which centered on functional safety, self-driving demanded both at once. At NVIDIA I experienced a full-scale effort to confront the long tail of autonomous driving, pairing a data-driven end-to-end learning pipeline (Alpamayo) with a rigorously engineered safety architecture (NDAS) operating in symbiotic tandem.

While such hybrid approaches define the present, robotics is rapidly moving toward large-scale learned systems. My current research explores how techniques from LLM safety and interpretability can be adapted to robot foundation models to make their behavior more transparent, predictable, and ultimately suitable for safety-critical deployment. This direction embodies the intersection of capability and safety that I hope to advance.

To continue contributing to capable and safe autonomy at scale, I want to build deeper foundations in robot learning, control, optimization, and safety-critical systems – areas that my formal education have touched only lightly, despite the practical exposure I’ve gained through industry and research. The Stanford EE MS offers the rigorous theoretical grounding and breadth I am seeking, and the Honors Cooperative Program would allow me to immediately connect this academic training with my full-time work on NVIDIA’s autonomous vehicle active-safety team. This structure is ideal for developing simultaneously as a researcher-practitioner and as an engineer contributing to a real safety-critical deployment pipeline.

Specifically, courses such as **EE 260B (Principles of Robot Autonomy II)** and **EE 381 (Sensorimotor Learning for Embodied Agents)** would deepen my understanding of the learning-based methods driving recent advances in robot capability. Meanwhile, **CS 238 (Decision Making Under Uncertainty)** and **AA 203 (Optimal and Learning-Based Control)** align directly with my focus on autonomous system safety.

Stanford’s EE department also has an exceptional concentration of faculty whose work aligns closely with my interests. **Prof. Dorsa Sadigh’s** research on safe decision-making and human–robot interaction parallels my focus on reliability in safety-critical autonomy. **Prof. Chelsea Finn’s** work on generalizable robot learning from large-scale, real-world data relates to my experience building learning-based perception systems and aligns with my vision of achieving robust, general-purpose robot capability. **Prof. Shuran Song’s** research on 3D perception and manipulation complements my background in multimodal sensing and scene understanding. I also hope to draw on perspectives outside EE, such as **Prof. Marco Pavone’s** work on long-tail robustness and safety-aware planning for autonomous vehicles, which closely informs the challenges I encounter in industry.

My long-term aim is to develop robotic systems that are not only broadly capable, but also transparent, reliable, and ready for real-world deployment. Stanford’s EE MS, especially through the HCP format, offers the depth, structure, and research environment that would complement the practical challenges I work on at NVIDIA. I look forward to growing at this intersection and contributing to the next generation of robust, scalable autonomous systems.

Despite having essentially no experience, I lucked into a research internship, working on deep learning-based COVID-19 diagnosis at the height of the pandemic under the supervision of Prof. Pengcheng Xi and Prof. Alexander Wong (very grateful that they took a chance on me!). Medical deep learning faces many of the same problems that robot learning does. The data is difficult to collect, often imbalanced, and reliability is especially important. To combat these problems, I worked on self-supervised pre-training, imbalance-focused loss functions, as well as model trustworthiness quantification. I studied on nights and weekends to make up the fact that I was a freshman with no previous ML experience. It was here that I first learned of both the power and difficulties of deep learning – while incredibly effective and useful, reliability in the real world remained a struggle. I then accepted an offer at a startup to work on similar deep learning problems; however, they rescinded my offer due to economic conditions right before final exams, forcing me to scramble to find another internship within 2 weeks (and making a big dent on my 1B term GPA!). Prof. Wong was gracious enough to take me back under his wing to work on object detection for warehouse robotic bin picking. Here, I saw those same issues, projected to the robotics space; the long tail once again reared its ugly head, as our models worked on the majority of objects but struggled with corner cases. This time, I prototyped a Mixture-of-Experts approach, automatically training smaller expert models to handle difficult the corner cases.




I ventured out to San Francisco to join Polymath Robotics, a startup building autonomous industrial vehicles. There, I continued to work on learning-based methods for 3D object detection (LiDAR and multi-modal). At the time, Polymath had no such existing system, so I did everything from implementing and training models to building a data collection pipeline and writing ROS drivers. My experience at Polymath continued drilling into me the difficulty of robotics and learning; the real world is just so complex that building capable, safe systems is a Herculean task. (I once had to drive out to a tomato farm in rural California to fix a tractor that had been struck by lighting!)

At this stage, I wanted something new. I had continued to work on both the COVID-19 and bin picking research projects part-time even when working full-time across the continent, and the toil of AI research was taking a toll. While I enjoyed the academic process (reading papers, conducting experiments, and writing about my findings), I didn't like the academia machine itself. I didn't like the tedium of the research process; I didn't like the constant pace of catching up to the state-of-the-art. I began experiencing burnout; my daily habit of paper reading slowed down, until I was barely reading papers at all. With the combination of my disillusionment of academia and the enjoyable time I had working at a startup, I thought it would be unlikely that I would be pursuing graduate school; my grades had already slipped during my 1B term due to the job rescission, so I decided that passing is good enough ("C's get degrees" is uttered thousands of times per day around Waterloo due to the school's heavy industry focus) and to focus on industry opportunities instead of academia.

Thus, the following term I returned to Polymath for my fourth internship. For the first time, I elected to work outside of AI. The Polymath team suggested that I help them build up their safety stack. This intrigued me; I had experienced the power of deep learning but also seen its pitfalls when it came to reliability – so what does actual safety in the real world look like? Working on the safety system, which had to be compliant to the ISO 26262 safety standard, showed me how stringent safety standards are in the real world; it's not just scoring above a certain number on some evaluation dataset, but an entire development lifecycle of functional safety. While this safety stuff was interesting and illuminating, I felt an itch for deep learning and research, came back as robot learning began picking up steam around this time. I decided that perhaps graduate school would be the way to go after all; life is short, and there's simply too many cool things to learn! I re-focused on my academic performance; since fall 2023, I've only scored below 87/100 for one course (87 is required the average for Dean's List). Even during my exchange term, where courses were counted on a credit/no-credit basis and not factored into GPA, I got the highest mark for Intelligent Robotics and Embodied AI.

For my 5th and 6th internships at NVIDIA, I combined my experience in robot perception and safety to help build the active safety system for NVIDIA's autonomous vehicle partnership with Mercedes-Benz. For the first time, I witness a full-scale effort at fighting the long tail of self-driving (the long march of 9s, as Andrej Karpathy put), with a ML data flywheel for end-to-end learning and classical safety system in symbiotic tandem. I am fortunate to be returning for a full-time role in 2026, where I hope to help NVIDIA make capable, safety, and useful self-driving a reality.. 

Then, why pursue an EE MS at Stanford if I'm already working on capable, safe, and useful robotics in the industry? First, I believe I have a lot of learning left to do. Despite all my experience in deep learning and sensor fusion, I haven't learned about either in formal capacity, as my undergraduate program doesn't have any technical electives before 5th year. The EE MS program has wonderful breadth and depth of coursework that is relevant to robotics – I can hone my skills in a ton of areas. I also think that while robotics is beginning to transition from an academic trifle to the real world, there is still much more research advances until capable, safe, and useful robots become a reality. I believe that the EE MS will help me develop my research and development acumen in these cutting-edge topics to be able to effectively contribute to my goal.

\textbf{Deep learning research.} My earliest exposure to machine learning came through COVID-19 medical imaging research under Prof. Pengcheng Xi and Prof. Alexander Wong. Medical datasets are small and class imbalanced, so standard deep learning methods struggle on rare but clinically important cases and tend to learn biased decision boundaries. Our work [1][2][3] combined self supervised pre-training, imbalance aware loss functions, and trustworthiness quantification, and our model was deployed across 15 hospitals. I contributed to model design and training, and led much of the interpretability and trustworthiness evaluation that helped clinicians understand model outputs. This experience showed me that deep learning can deliver real impact in high-stakes settings, yet is often limited by data scarcity and long-tailed behavior.

\vspace{0.1in}

I carried these lessons into bin picking research with Prof. Wong, where I experimented with Mixture of Experts methods that train specialist models for long tail cases [4]. Even with corner cases explicitly targeted and synthetic data in a controlled environment, improving robustness remained difficult and often exposed new failure modes when others were fixed. These themes continued on the WATonomous, the self-driving team at Waterloo, supervised by Dr. Mohammad Al-Sharman, where I developed trajectory prediction and 3D perception components for our self driving vehicle. For example, we designed a bespoke 3D flow method to support dynamic 3D reconstruction, only to have it fail systematically because of ground segmentation errors, underscoring that robustness is not just a matter of scaling models. This pushed me toward the question that now motivates my work: how can we build robotic systems that operate safely in the open world despite gaps between development and deployment?

On validation scenarios, this significantly reduced automatic emergency braking false positives while maintaining high recall for true hazards.
